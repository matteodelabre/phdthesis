\section{Comparison and perspectives}

This chapter presented two approaches generalizing bilateral reconciliation to contexts where several dependent units evolve jointly.
This situation arises when multiple genes evolve together along a genome.
The general principle consists in integrating an intermediate level into the evolutionary hierarchy, describing the evolution of adjacencies (DeCo) or of segments carrying sets of units (super-reconciliation).

The DeCo approach makes it possible to model events that affect pairs of neighbouring units, notably including rearrangements.
From an algorithmic point of view, it consists in individually reconciling the phylogenetic trees of each unit, then gathering pairs of nodes to form an adjacency forest.
Each root of this forest corresponds to the gain of an adjacency that then propagates to its descendants.
The objective is to minimize the number of roots, that is, the number of adjacency gains.
The complexity status of the problem is still unknown, although it has been conjectured to be NP-complete, but a polynomial algorithm exists when the individual reconciliations are fixed in advance.
Modelling at the scale of adjacencies imposes limits on the events that can be taken into account, since it excludes events that simultaneously affect more than two units.
Moreover, an optimal result may contain non-linear structures, in which a unit is a neighbour of at least three others, that are incompatible with the linear or circular structures of genomes.

Super-reconciliation offers a similar paradigm, based on the evolution of segments joining an arbitrary number of units.
This extension makes it possible to model segmental events affecting several units.
In the same way as for DeCo, the algorithmic objective is to build a tree in which each node gathers segments of nodes coming from individual reconciliations.
The structure reconstructed here is a tree and not a forest, since it is assumed that all segments derive from a unique ancestral segment.
The associated combinatorial optimization problem is NP-complete in its general version, when the topology of the synteny tree is unknown.
If the topology is fixed, the complexity is unknown for the variant of the problem with segments, and polynomial for its variant with groups.

Previously, super-reconciliation was limited to the \(\recmodelDL\) event model.
In the remainder of this manuscript, we will show how this approach can be extended up to the \(\recmodelDTLx\) model, by integrating horizontal transfer events and by taking into account interactions with unsampled hosts.
We will also study the potential of this extended model for analysing the evolution of biological systems through several applications.
